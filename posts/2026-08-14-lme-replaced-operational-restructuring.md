There used to be a sequence. A company ran out of room, someone did the work of
figuring out why, and the capital structure got rebuilt around whatever the
business could actually support. The financial fix was downstream of the
operating diagnosis.

That sequence is mostly gone. The financial fix now happens first, fast, and
often instead.

## The numbers say the exchange is the default

Moody's put roughly **65% of private credit defaults in 2025** in the distressed
restructuring bucket. Not chapter 11 — exchanges. The measured default rate for
private credit last year sits anywhere between **1.6% and 4.7%** depending only
on whether you count those exchanges as defaults at all.

That range is the whole story in one statistic. The dominant resolution
mechanism in credit is now a transaction that reasonable people disagree about
whether to call a default.

## What an LME actually buys

Maturity runway and a lower cash coupon. That's it. Both real, both valuable,
neither one an answer to why the business stopped generating cash.

An operational restructuring is slow and unpleasant: you close locations, you
exit product lines, you fight with management about a cost base they built. It
takes quarters and it makes enemies. An uptier takes six weeks, needs a
majority rather than consensus, and pays fees to the people who execute it.

Given those two options and a sponsor whose equity is out of the money, the
choice makes itself. Nobody in the room is incentivized to run the slow one.

## The bill arrives as recidivism

Moody's counted **repeat offenders as roughly 40% of US corporate defaults in
2023**. That number is the price of treating a leverage problem as a maturity
problem.

The mechanism isn't complicated:

1. Company can't service debt because the business is impaired.
2. LME cuts cash interest and pushes maturities out two or three years.
3. Nothing changes operationally, because nothing in the deal required it.
4. Two or three years later, less liquidity, worse docs, fewer assets left
   unencumbered, and a lender group that has already been through one fight.

Round two is always worse than round one. The easy collateral has been pledged,
the unrestricted subsidiary capacity has been used, and the cooperative lenders
have already been sorted from the excluded ones.

## What I'd actually watch

Not the LME announcement. The twelve months after it.

If the exchange comes with a genuine operating plan — new management, closed
footprint, a cost program with dates attached — treat it as a bridge. If it
comes with a press release about "enhanced financial flexibility" and no change
to how the company is run, price it as a delay and start diligencing the next
capital structure now.

The second one is more common, and the market keeps paying full price for it.
