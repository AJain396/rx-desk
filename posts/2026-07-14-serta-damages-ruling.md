On July 7, 2026, Judge Christopher Lopez in the Southern District of Texas entered judgment against the participating lenders in Serta. The headline number is roughly $400 million. The judgment itself is $261.13 million; the rest is New York statutory prejudgment interest, running at 9% since June 22, 2020.

Sit with that split for a second. Nearly a third of the exposure is just the clock.

## The liability half is now settled law in the Fifth Circuit

The 2020 exchange was not an "open market purchase." The court treated it as a payment in respect of principal, which triggered the pro rata sharing provision, which the participating lenders didn't satisfy.

This is the reading the excluded lenders have been pressing since 2020, and it has now survived all the way through to a damages award. The idea that an open-market-purchase exception can cover a negotiated, non-ratable exchange among a hand-picked majority is finished, at least in this circuit.

## The damages half is where the lesson is

The court rejected the models built off Serta's 2023 chapter 11 emergence and valued the breach as of the breach date, marking the first lien loans at 25 cents.

That choice does two things. It caps the claim at what the position was worth in June 2020, instead of letting excluded lenders capture the full downstream consequences of the transaction. And it front-loads the clock, because a 2020 breach date means six years of 9% statutory interest before anyone gets paid.

The second effect swamps the first. New York prejudgment interest isn't discretionary and it isn't compounded at some market rate. It's 9% simple, mandatory, and it accrues whether or not the litigation moves quickly. In a dispute that took six years, that turns a $261m judgment into a $400m one.

## Two structural details worth noting

Each defendant is on the hook only for its proportional share of first lien holdings as of June 22, 2020 — there's no joint liability. A participating lender's exposure is a function of its own ticket, not the group's, which changes the settlement dynamics: there's no single deep pocket to chase, and no contribution fight among defendants.

Breach-date valuation cuts both ways, too. Excluded lenders who assumed damages would be measured against a reorganized enterprise got substantially less than they modeled. Anyone underwriting uptier litigation as a recovery strategy should be running breach-date marks, not emergence-date ones.

## Where it goes

Expect the Fifth Circuit again. But the practical effect is already priced into how deals get done: the open-market-purchase exception is now a drafted, fought-over provision rather than boilerplate, and getting it wrong has a public number attached to it for the first time.

That number is the useful part. Not the precedent. The invoice.
