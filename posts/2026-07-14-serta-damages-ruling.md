On **July 7, 2026**, Judge Christopher Lopez in the Southern District of Texas
entered judgment against the participating lenders in Serta. The headline number
is roughly **$400 million**. The judgment itself is **$261.13 million**, and the
rest is New York statutory prejudgment interest running at **9% since June 22,
2020**.

Sit with that split for a second. Nearly a third of the exposure is the clock.

## The liability half is now settled law in the Fifth Circuit

The 2020 exchange was not an "open market purchase." The court treated it as a
payment in respect of principal, which triggered the pro rata sharing provision,
which the participating lenders did not satisfy.

This is the reading the excluded lenders have been pressing since 2020, and it
has now survived all the way through to a damages award. The defense that an
open-market-purchase exception can carry a negotiated, non-ratable exchange
among a hand-picked majority is finished — at least in this circuit.

## The damages half is where the lesson is

The court rejected the models built off Serta's 2023 chapter 11 emergence and
valued the breach as of the breach date, marking the first lien loans at **25
cents**.

That choice does two things:

- It **caps the claim** at what the position was worth in June 2020, rather than
  letting excluded lenders capture the full downstream consequences of the
  transaction.
- It **front-loads the clock**, because a 2020 breach date means six years of 9%
  statutory interest before anyone gets paid.

The second effect swamps the first. New York prejudgment interest is not
discretionary and it is not compounded at some market rate — it is 9% simple,
mandatory, and it accrues whether or not the litigation moves quickly. In a
dispute that took six years, that turns a $261m judgment into a $400m one.

## Two structural details worth noting

**No joint liability.** Each defendant is on the hook for its proportional share
of first lien holdings as of June 22, 2020. A participating lender's exposure is
a function of its own ticket, not the group's. That materially changes the
settlement dynamics — there's no single deep pocket to chase, and no
contribution fight among defendants.

**Breach-date valuation cuts both ways.** Excluded lenders who assumed damages
would be measured against a reorganized enterprise got substantially less than
they modeled. Anyone underwriting uptier litigation as a recovery strategy should
be running breach-date marks, not emergence-date ones.

## Where it goes

Expect the Fifth Circuit again. But the practical effect is already priced into
how deals get done: the open-market-purchase exception is now a drafted, fought-over
provision rather than boilerplate, and the cost of getting it wrong has a public
number attached to it for the first time.

That number is the useful part. Not the precedent — the invoice.
