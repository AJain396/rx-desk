1/7. Volkswagen (VW) is looking to cut about 1/7 of its workforce by the end of the decade. Yet, nobody has moved to mention Chapter 11.

Earlier this month, on September 3, VW’s supervisory board voted unanimously for its “Future Plan 2030”. The plan adds an additional 50,000 job cuts on top of programs implemented in 2024 and 2025. In total, the firm is looking to about 100,000 job cuts. That’s roughly 15% of their 652,000 employees.

I choose to mention Chapter 11 because what’s driven this cut policy didn’t happen overnight. It’s been a long-coming trend.

- December, 2024: VW and its unions agreed to 35,000 job cuts in Germany over 5 years
- June, 2026: The German target for cuts jumped to 50,000 by 2030
- September, 2026: The board admitted that European capacity was over 500,000 units above demand and that the four German plants don’t have guaranteed production lined up for after current models end between 2031-2034.

Then on September 18, VW cut its 2026 margin guidance to "up to 1 percent," mostly because of roughly €10 billion of one-off charges. This is exactly why we’re not speaking about “bankruptcy”.

VW’s problem is not its debt. At the end of June, 2026, the firm had €32.75 billion of net liquidity. Chapter 11 looks to deal with liabilities, but VW doesn’t have a liability issue. Rather, its issues lie on the operating end as there is more factory capacity and more employees than it can support. Cheaper, imported cars like those from Chinese competition are no aid to operations.

That's the side of restructuring we don't hear about as much: operational restructuring. For VW, it means there aren't lenders to negotiate with or haircuts to hand out. Instead, it's a slow process of cutting capacity and headcount, mostly through partial retirement programs.

It's also not VW's first "most radical plan ever." In 2016, after the diesel scandal, VW and its unions agreed to cut 30,000 jobs at the core brand in exchange for no forced layoffs in Germany until 2025. A decade later, we're back with more than three times the number.

VW can cut its way to a better margin, but it can't cut its way into a business model that works against BYD.

So that one in seven number is a huge restructuring, just not the kind that ends up in a courtroom.
