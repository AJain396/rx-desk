Railroads are where U.S. restructuring started. The original distressed credit. In the 1800s, failing railroads went through court-run receiverships, and the Bankruptcy Code still has a section just for them: Subchapter IV of Chapter 11. We see a few big differences from a normal case:

- Trustee: there's no debtor in possession. The Secretary of Transportation sends a list of five “disinterested” people, and the U.S. Trustee appoints one of them to run the case (§1163).
- Public interest: the court and trustee have to weigh it next to creditors (§1165), and a plan only gets confirmed if it's "consistent with the public interest" (§1173).
- Injury claims: personal injury and death claims from running the railroad get paid as administrative expenses, whether they arose before or after the filing (§1171).

Now… Brightline. Subchapter IV only applies in a case "concerning a railroad" (§103(h)), and the Code defines a railroad as the common carrier itself, or an owner of track leased to one (§101(44)). In Brightline’s case, the entities that filed are non-operating parents. Brightline Trains Florida LLC, which runs the trains, actually stayed out.

That means, as filed right now, the parents run their own case as debtors in possession. There’s no list from the DOT and no mandatory trustee (unless one of the parents owns track the Opco leases). If the RSA falls apart and the Opco ha to file, then these pieces from Subchapter IV would come into play. Leaving the Opco out for now keeps the Series 2024 bonds and Assured’s insurance in play, definitely the plan.

There's an older playbook here. When the Texas and Pacific Railway (T&P) went into receivership in December 1885, it had about $43.8 million of mortgage debt and wasn't earning enough to cover its interest. The 1888 reorganization mostly kept the old creditors in, just with new paper:

- New bonds: $25 million of 5% first mortgage bonds and $25 million of income bonds (coupon paid if there are sufficient earnings), with better-secured creditors getting more of the first mortgage ones.
- The catch: those income bonds weren't due until December 1, 2000.
- The land: the railway's Texas land went into the Texas Pacific Land Trust, and its certificates went to holders of certain T&P bonds.

Brightline's RSA looks a lot closer to that than to a real haircut. The Muni holders keep their bonds at full principal, senior holders can push interest out, and Assured, which already insures the senior bonds, is putting in new money. Brightline is buying time which only works if ridership catches up before the can runs out again.
