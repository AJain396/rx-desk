Everyone calls it a wall. Which is the wrong word, because it puts the emphasis on height, and height was never the issue here.

## The size, first

Start with the numbers, because they're the part people get wrong. More than $700 billion of US high yield matures across 2027–2029, over $350 billion of that landing in 2029 alone, and if you throw in leveraged loans and speculative-grade maturities, 2028 by itself runs to something like $1 trillion. Those are big figures and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But 2025 high yield issuance cleared $325 billion, up 17%, the strongest year since 2021 — and on pure volume, the market absorbs this without blinking. Three years of ordinary issuance against three years of maturities. If this were only a capacity question, we'd be done here.

It's not a capacity question.

## The composition is the whole problem

Two numbers from 2025, sitting next to each other, that should make you uneasy. Over 70% of issuance was refinancing, the highest share since 2009. Straight CCC paper: under 2% of issuance, second-lowest on record. Put those next to each other and the story writes itself. The market spent all of 2025 refinancing everything that could be refinanced, and touched almost nothing rated CCC while doing it. Which means what's sitting in front of 2027 and 2028 isn't a random sample of the credit universe. It's what's left over, the names that tried to term out into a wide-open market and couldn't.

## Why that matters more than the total

A wall of BB paper is a calendar problem. A wall built from three years of "everyone who could refinance already did" is something else entirely, a default pipeline wearing a scheduling problem's name. The issuers still standing in front of 2027 are there for a reason, usually one of two: they tried and the market said no, or they saw the clearing spread and decided to wait it out. Waiting doesn't fix either problem.

## What actually breaks first

Not the maturity date on the calendar. Long before that: springing covenants, cash sweeps that trigger 12 to 18 months out, and, quietly, in parallel, an auditor asking uncomfortable going-concern questions. That's where the real clock starts, which means the actual distress shows up through late 2026 and 2027, not on the day a bond technically comes due. By the time you're looking at a maturity date, the outcome already got decided, a year earlier, in a room you weren't in.

## The practical read

Forget the aggregate. Track three things instead: the ratings distribution of what's still outstanding by maturity year, how many refi attempts get launched and then pulled before pricing, and how much extension activity shows up, because that's the tell that a full refinancing wasn't actually on the table.

The size was never the story. Who's left in front of it is.
