## Part 1: what happened

Start with the number. CSC Holdings, Optimum's operating subsidiary, has $21.8 billion of funded debt, $6.2 billion of it due in 2027 and $4.1 billion in April 2027 alone.

Creditors saw Drahi coming. In June 2024, investors that claim to hold about 99% of that debt signed a cooperation agreement: nobody cuts a side deal with the company alone.

Drahi's response? Sue them.

- In November 2025, Optimum sued Apollo, Ares, Oaktree and other creditors, calling the co-op an "illegal cartel" built to lock it out of the credit market. The lenders moved to dismiss, and Wall Street trade groups lined up behind them. I found no ruling.
- He also borrowed around them: a $2 billion loan at a 9% fixed rate to subsidiaries outside the lenders' collateral, due November 2028.

Then came June 1. Optimum moved Optimum East and its 50.01% of Lightpath into a new unrestricted subsidiary, CSC Investments II LLC ("Unsub Topco"). Unsub Topco raised $300 million of preferred equity from institutions and swapped $200 million more to Drahi's holding company for his common stock, at $2.50 a share. The preferred pays 13% in cash, or 15% if paid in kind. And if CSC ends up in a non-consensual Chapter 11, holders get 2.5x their money back.

Here's the math that matters. By the company's own numbers, Optimum East did about $2.0 billion of 2025 adjusted EBITDA. The restricted group, the part lenders can still reach, did about $1.0 billion against $21.8 billion of debt.

Now each side has a weapon. Optimum says a restructuring that deconsolidates CSC for tax purposes would trigger a tax bill of more than $4 billion. Lenders can argue the June moves were a fraudulent transfer.

**If the co-op holds:** a negotiated deal, probably with some debt swapped for equity, and the lawsuit dropped. **If it cracks:** Drahi gets what he wanted all along. He picks off lenders one by one, trades them into better-ranked paper at a discount, and pushes out maturities while keeping the equity. That's his best case, and it's my read, not reporting.

## Part 2: was this predictable?

Honestly? Mostly.

- **The playbook:** Altice planned to fund the Cablevision deal with $8.6 billion of new debt at CSC Holdings, and roll over $3.1 billion of existing CSC debt and $2.8 billion at Cablevision.
- **The track record:** in February 2025, Altice France agreed with creditors to cut about €24.8 billion of debt to about €16.9 billion. Creditors took 45% of the equity. The detail that matters for Optimum: as part of that deal, Altice France agreed to put previously unrestricted assets back under lenders' collateral. The deal closed on October 1, 2025. So Drahi has seen lenders force an unsub unwind before. In the U.S., he moved first.
- **The rates:** the debt was mostly fixed-rate notes. Only the term loans and revolvers floated off LIBOR. So the damage wasn't rising coupons. It was refinancing. By the end of 2022, the market valued the fixed-rate debt at about $12.5 billion against a $17.3 billion carrying value. Anything issued near zero percent eventually has to roll at today's rates, and new money now costs 9%.

Cheap money was never permanent. Drahi's willingness to leave lenders holding the bag was never a secret. The timing was the only real surprise.

## Deal Parties

- **Borrower:** Optimum Communications, Inc. (f/k/a Altice USA) / CSC Holdings, LLC; unrestricted sub CSC Investments II LLC. No bankruptcy filing.
- **Financial Advisors / Bankers:** Company: Evercore, placement agent on the June preferred; Altman Solon, industry consultant. Moelis was reported as company advisor in January 2025, not reconfirmed since. Co-op: PJT Partners.
- **Major Creditors / Creditor Groups:** Co-op including Apollo, Ares, BlackRock and Oaktree (named defendants); the full list is on the docket.
- **Law Firms:** Company: White & Case and Quinn Emanuel on the June transactions; Kellogg Hansen on the antitrust suit. Co-op: Akin Gump; Sullivan & Cromwell for defendants in the antitrust case.
