Chapter 11 is supposed to be the last resort. A big red button. Lately, it seems like nobody wants to press that button.

In 2025, we saw that roughly 65% of corporate defaults (tracked by Moody's) were distressed restructurings. However, the “average” distressed company today isn’t filing. Instead, it’s running a liability management exercise (LME) and trying to fix its balance sheet without the complexity of a judge.

It’s pretty clear as to why: bankruptcy “costs” a lot more than we see at face value. We see two groups:

- Direct costs: professional fees can run 4 to 4.5 percent of the bankrupt firms' assets and liabilities. The share shrinks as companies get bigger, but still a considerable amount.
- Indirect costs: equity often gets wiped out, so a PE sponsor, for example, loses everything. Management is forced to spend time in court, rather than on the struggling business. We also see damage to a reputation that can’t be fixed with a snap: customers and vendors get nervous and every detail ends up in public eye.

An LME skips most of this. Only lenders that the documents require are needed and our sponsor keeps equity. However, the LME doesn’t fix the businesses. It just buys time as we’ll see.

How it works:

- Drop-down (J.Crew, 2016–17): In December 2016, J.Crew moved a 72.04% ownership interest in its U.S. trademarks to a subsidiary outside the reach of lenders. In July 2017, it swapped $565.7 million of holding-company PIK notes for $249.6 million of 13% secured notes plus preferred and common stock, and moved the remaining 27.96% of the IP over. J.Crew filed Chapter 11 anyway in May 2020, handing control to its lenders.
- Uptier (Serta, 2020): A majority of lenders swapped into new debt ranking above everyone who wasn't invited. Serta filed Chapter 11 in January, 2023, and the Fifth Circuit later held that the swap was not a permitted "open market purchase" and breached the other lenders' right to pro rata treatment.
- Asset shuffle (DISH, 2024). In January 2024, DISH Network moved wireless spectrum licenses up to EchoStar, and DISH DBS placed about 3.0 million DISH TV subscribers in a newly unrestricted subsidiary. In June 2026, DISH DBS filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 backed by holders of more than 88% of its notes. The company's stated reason: the AT&T wireless spectrum license sale hadn't closed, so DBS couldn't repay $2.0 billion of notes due July 1.

J. Crew, Serta, and DISH all ended up in court. We’re seeing a pattern there. S&P found re-defaulters comprised 42% of defaulters in Q1 2025, the highest share since early 2019. LMEs might fix debt terms, but they never touch the broken cogs of the business.

So we still end with that “original” last resort. Just later, after a "creative" transaction, and usually by a company with less left to save.
