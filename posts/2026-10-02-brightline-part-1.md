Brightline exists as the only privately owned and operated intercity passenger railroad in the U.S. In September, part of it filed for Chapter 11.

Now, it was a big risk from the start. The Miami–Orlando line took roughly $6 billion to build. In 2025, it carried 3.1 million riders and brought in $214 million of revenue, which was about one third of what had been projected.

- I don’t think any of this is surprising really. Beyond any quantitative analysis, the thought of an entirely new rail system seems not only capital intensive, but also complicated in terms of attracting consumers well-accustomed to driving/flying.

In April 2024, when it rated the new bonds, KBRA expected about 760,000 long-distance riders and Brightline's own forecast was over 2.5 million. This year, rider count and revenue are both up 14% through August, which is good news, just not "$6 billion" good.

The big issue is that Brightline is, well… private. Amtrak has Congress sending it money every year, whereas Brightline only had Fortress (its sponsor) and a stack of bondholders, and they're the ones absorbing the shortfall.

Here’s how the debt is stacked:

- Opco senior: $2.2 billion of Series 2024 tax-exempt bonds, issued through the Florida Development Finance Corporation (FDFC). Assured Guaranty insures just over half.
- Holdco: $985 million of Series 2025B FDFC bonds at Brightline Florida Holdings.
- AAF Operations Holdings: $925 million of Series 2024 and $285.7 million of Series 2024A tax-exempt bonds.
- Corporate notes: about $1.1 billion at an 11% coupon, trading around 10 cents in May.

FDFC is a conduit issuer. It issues the bonds, but Brightline pays them back, so the state isn't on the hook.

The rough piece: the $2.2 billion Series 2024 bonds are private activity bonds, tax-free to holders because Brightline qualified under Section 142 of the tax code. Under Treasury regulation 1.150-3, cutting principal (or changing the terms enough) would make each one count as retired and reissued for tax purposes. The "new" bond would start from scratch in earning its tax-exempt status.

That seems to explain the plan:

- Who filed: a group of parent companies that don't run any trains, while the operating company stays out of Chapter 11.They plan to continue operations, this isn’t the end of their story.
- The Munis: untouched. All four series above stay outstanding at full principal.
- New money: $490 million to the Opco ($140 million senior, $350 million junior), plus up to $178 million of post-petition funding from Assured.

We’d guess the plan was built around who holds the paper. Bloomberg named Invesco, Nuveen and First Eagle among the senior Muni holders. Funds like those can't walk away holding new equity or taxable debt. So any loss has to land on someone else, and the corporate notes (trading around 10 cents in May) aren't on the press release's "remains outstanding" list.

For now, the Miami–Orlando trains are still running, and Muni holders get to hang on to their tax exemption.

## Deal Parties

- Debtor(s): certain Brightline parent (non-operating) entities, Bankr. D.N.J. The operating company, Brightline Trains Florida LLC, did not file.
- Financial Advisors / Bankers: Company: Perella Weinberg Partners and Houlihan Lokey (investment bankers), Alvarez & Marsal (financial advisor). Assured Guaranty: Lazard. Ad hoc group of mutual fund bondholders: GLC Advisors.
- Major Creditors / Creditor Groups: Assured Guaranty (insures slightly over 50% of the opco senior bonds); ad hoc group of mutual fund bondholders. Senior Muni holders reported by Bloomberg include Invesco, Nuveen and First Eagle.
- Law Firms: Company: Skadden, Arps, Slate, Meagher & Flom and Cole Schotz. Assured Guaranty: Milbank. Mutual fund group: Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer. Claims agent: Stretto.
